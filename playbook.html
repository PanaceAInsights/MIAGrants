The playbook

Which door to knock on, in what order, and what to do at the point where most Australian research quietly dies.

1. Before you publish anything

This is the only irreversible decision in the whole process, and it comes first.

File before you publish. Europe never forgives. Australia, the United States, Japan and Canada give you a twelve-month grace period for your own disclosure. Europe and China give you nothing. Publish first and you may rescue your Australian rights while permanently losing Europe on the same invention.

One trap inside the trap: the Australian grace period requires a complete or PCT application within twelve months of the disclosure. Filing only a provisional does not invoke it.

A conference abstract counts. A poster counts. A thesis uploaded to a repository counts. A preprint counts. Talk to your commercialisation office before you submit, not after.

2. Understand what filing actually buys you

A provisional application costs A$100 in official fees and roughly A$5,000 to A$8,000 attorney-drafted. It buys a priority date and twelve months. That is all it buys.

This is why the national filing statistics look healthy and why universities file freely: filing is nearly free. In 2025 Australians filed 6,867 provisionals, up 58%, the highest since 2008. But 52% were filed without a patent attorney, and independent analysis of IP Australia's own open data shows the standard patent acceptance rate is 22.6% for self-filed applications against 53.2% for attorney-represented ones, with over 90% of self-filed provisionals resulting in no further action.

Filing is not the bottleneck. It never was.

3. The cliff

The bottleneck is prosecution: taking an application through PCT and then national phase to grant, in each jurisdiction you care about, and then paying renewals for twenty years.

Renewal fees escalate roughly eight-fold across a patent's life, from A$345 at year six to A$2,815 at year nineteen. A patent gets more expensive the longer you hold it, which is why portfolios get pruned.

A university will file a hundred patents. It will not prosecute a hundred patents. It triages, and most filings are quietly allowed to lapse. The decision lands back on the inventor, usually around month twelve or month thirty, and usually with very little warning.

4. Four options at the cliff edge

  1. Let it lapse. The default. Costs nothing, ends everything. This includes losing it by accident: publishing before the complete application is filed takes the priority date with it.
  2. Convince the institution to fund prosecution. Possible and genuinely difficult. You are competing against every other disclosure in the portfolio, and the institution is making a portfolio decision, not a judgement about your science.
  3. Find a partner or backer early. Licence it, or bring in an industry partner who funds prosecution because they want the asset. See the Investors tab, and note that corporate partnering teams are usually a better door than corporate venture arms.
  4. Spin out and prosecute it yourself. Your company bears the cost and the risk. The hard road, and the only one that changes the negotiation.

5. Why option four changes the terms

If the institution funds the filing, the prosecution, the attorneys and the commercialisation effort, then the institution is carrying the risk. A modest inventor share follows from that, and it is what you should expect.

If your spin-out carries prosecution and commercialisation of an asset the institution declined to fund, you are no longer asking. You are negotiating, and you have something real to negotiate with.

The IP conversation is not really about fairness. It is about who is holding the risk when the invoice arrives.

This is also why the CRC model works so well. It separates background IP that each party already owns from new project IP created together, and it does so before anyone spends money. If you are structuring any collaboration, steal that framework.

6. If you form a company, three things unlock immediately

  • The R&D Tax Incentive. 43.5% of eligible R&D refunded in cash to a loss-making company under A$20 million turnover. This is an entitlement, not a competition, and it is often the largest non-dilutive funding a spin-out has. Radium Capital will advance it so you get the cash during the year rather than waiting.
  • Company-only grant schemes. NSW Medical Devices Fund (A$500,000 to A$5 million), ANDHealth+ (up to A$5 million, non-dilutive, no equity), CRC-Projects, MVP Ventures. None of these are available to a university researcher without an entity.
  • Your university's pre-seed fund. Sydney, UNSW and Monash all now run one, all up to A$500,000 to A$1 million, and Sydney's accepts expressions of interest year-round with a response in one to two weeks.

8. Choosing jurisdictions is a cost decision

You cannot afford every country, and that is the point. The United States spends US$5.3 trillion a year on healthcare at 18% of GDP. Australia spends A$270.5 billion at 10%.

The regulatory traffic runs one way: the TGA will accept market authorisation evidence from comparable overseas regulators as supporting evidence, but the FDA gives you nothing for being approved in Australia. And the FDA door is not only for giants, with 295 AI-enabled devices cleared in 2025 and 183 companies receiving their first ever authorisation that year.

9. Two structural gaps worth knowing

These are honest negative findings from the research behind this directory, and both are opportunities rather than just absences.

  • There is no dedicated health or medtech angel syndicate in Australia. The closest functional equivalents are Scale Investors' Care Tech focus and the investor networks attached to MedTech Actuator and ANDHealth.
  • There is no melanoma, dermatology or skin cancer commercialisation program in Australia or New Zealand, and the national health-AI accelerator has closed. Cicada's SPARC, built with Breakthrough T1D Australia for type 1 diabetes, is the proven template for what a disease-specific program looks like.
